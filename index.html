Welcome! I am Benjamin Gibson, a postdoc at STScI. My research focuses on studying the resolved and integrated light of stellar population in nearby galaxies as a probe of galactic assembly and evolution. I was born and raised in Charlotte, NC and did my undergraduate studies at Florida State University.

I also help organize the Galaxies/AGN Journal Club and ISM* Group Meetings at Space Telescope, bringing a wide variety of excellent researchers to share their work at the Institute!

Research

I am broadly interested in the assembly and evolution of disk galaxies, and developing new data analysis strategies for extracting chemical and kinematic information from data.

Galactic Structure

The distribution of mass (namely stars and gas, but also dark matter) in a galaxy contains a wealth of knowledge about its past. For example, if a galaxy has a highly structured disk (ie a strong bar and spiral arms), it is less likely to have had a recent significant merger to mix everything up.

The Alpha Bimodality

The Milky Way's disk is split into two components. One is made up of generally older stars that have less circular orbits that take them further from the galaxy's midplane. This is generally called the thick disk. The other component, naturally called the thin disk, is made up of generally younger stars on rather circular orbits much more confined to the midplane. This pattern exists in many disk galaxies, however in the Milky Way, this distinction goes further. The chemical makeup of these stars are rather different. The origin of this distinction is likely a result of the Milky Way's merger history, but we are not sure how common it is in other galaxies.

Integrated Light

In order to find this bimodality in other galaxies, I developed a new method to analyze APOGEE integrated-light spectra of the Andromeda Galaxy, M31. This method used the dinstinct kinmeatics of M31's overlapping stellar populations to decompose them within the spectra and independently characterize their chemistry. Establishing the prevalence of this structure throughout the universe is essential towards building more accurate, versatile models of galactic evolution.

Resolved Stars

At STScI, I have begun work on two projects analyzing resolved-star photometry of three perplexing nearby galaxies: NGC7793, NGC4449, and M82. These galaxies have a ton of dust and ongoing star formation, and their internal physics can be probed by studying their resolved stellar populations. By combining Hubble and James Webb, we have assembled panchromatic imagery of these galaxies, and by using the BEAST software, can measure the stellar parameters and dust extinction of millions of stars.

Publications

Gibson et al. (2023)

The Chemodynamics of the Stellar Populations in M31 from APOGEE Integrated Light Spectroscopy

The primary results from this paper are shown left. They are maps of the average kinematics, chemical composition, and age of the stellar populations in the disk and bulge of M31.

Gibson et al. (2025)

Towards understanding the Milky Way's typicality: assessing the chemodynamics of M31's bulge and bar, thick and thin discs

In this paper, I developed a new method to decompose spectra into kinematically hot and cool components and independently measure their chemistry. I applied this to a stacked spectrum of M31's northern disk and identified two chemically and kinematically distinct components that bear striking resemblence to the Milky Way's thin and thick disks.

Gibson et al. (2026)

Probing the Assembly of Triangulum: Precision Metallicities and α Abundances of M33's Star Clusters from APOGEE Integrated-Light Spectroscopy

I took more APOGEE integrated light spectra and measured the chemistry of 20 star clusters in M33's disk and halo. The halo clusters exhibit a spread in α abundance at constant metallicity that is similar to the Milky Way. I also found several clusters that are strong candidates for having been accreted into M33.

Gibson et al. (2026), in prep.

I am analyzing UV to IR photometry of ~400,000 and ~900,000 stars in NGC7793 and NGC4449, respectively. These galaxies are called BADGRs: Blue and Dusty Gas-Rich Sources, and are rather paradoxical. I am using the BEAST to measure and map the stellar and dust parameters in these galaxies to ascertain how they can be so UV-bright and have so much dust.
